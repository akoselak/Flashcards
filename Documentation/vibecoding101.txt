**1. Know what you want before you prompt**

Write two or three sentences on the behavior you want and how you'll know it works. 
Most slow vibe coding comes from vague prompts that generate the wrong thing, which you then have to untangle. 
In your flashcard app, "save my cards so they survive a refresh" is a good prompt because you can test it in 
ten seconds.

**2. Keep the loop small**

Prompt, run, test, commit. One feature per cycle, and never stack a new prompt on top of code you haven't run. 
Small steps make bugs easy to locate because you know the last change caused them. 
They also keep each piece of new code small enough to actually understand.

**3. Match your reading depth to the risk**

You don't need to understand every line equally. A rough triage:
- **Read closely:** anything touching data (saving, deleting, loading), anything with security implications, 
and anything that surprised you.
- **Skim for shape:** the overall structure. Which function does what, and where does data flow?
- **Skip for now:** styling details, boilerplate, and syntax you'll absorb over time.

**4. Make the AI teach as it builds**

These prompts cost a few seconds and pay off hugely:
- "Explain what changed and why, in plain language."
- "Walk me through this function line by line."
- "What's a simpler way to do this, and what are the tradeoffs?"
- "What could break here?"

A strong test is to explain a new piece of code back in your own words. If you can't, ask again until you can.

**5. Predict before you run**

Before testing a change, guess what will happen. 
Be specific: "after I add a card and refresh, it should still be there." 
When your prediction is wrong, that's exactly where your mental model has a gap, 
and it's the fastest learning signal you'll get.

**6. Change things by hand**

Make tiny edits yourself, like a color, a label, or a number in the quiz logic, and see what breaks. 
Hand edits build intuition that reading never does. 
Once a month, try to write one small function from scratch before asking for help.

**7. Debug with evidence**

When something breaks, give the AI the exact error message, what you expected, and what happened. 
Open your browser's developer console (F12) to find errors. 
Learning to read an error message is probably the single most valuable skill here, 
because it turns "it's broken" into a specific, fixable problem.

**8. Use git as your safety net**

Commit every time something works. Fearless experimentation is what makes fast iteration possible, 
and `git diff` shows exactly what the AI changed, which is a great way to review code without reading 
the whole file.

**9. Stay in charge of the design**

The AI is good at writing code and mediocre at deciding what the project should become. 
You decide the features, the structure, and when something is good enough. 
If you notice the AI piling on complexity (extra libraries, abstractions you didn't ask for), 
push back: "Make this simpler" is a legitimate and often best prompt.

**10. Keep a learning log**

A plain notes file with three headings works well: *what I asked*, *what I learned*, *what confused me*. 
Review the confusing items weekly and ask the AI to explain them. 
After a few weeks you'll see your own patterns and start prompting better.

**A quick weekly rhythm:**
- Build a new feature using the loop above.
- Pick one chunk of generated code and understand it fully, to the point where you could explain it to someone.
- Make one hand edit.
- Commit and log what you learned.

You'll go slower than pure "accept everything" vibe coding at first, 
but the understanding compounds quickly, and within a few weeks you'll be both faster and better at steering.
